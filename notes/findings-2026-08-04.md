# Demand Management ABM — Interim Findings

**2026-08-04. Preliminary.** All results replicated across 12–20 independent
seeds with 95% confidence intervals. Nothing here has been externally reviewed
and several hypotheses stated at the outset are now contradicted.

---

## 1. What the model does

A population of researchers, grouped into institutions, generates proposals. A
**demand-management mechanism** filters which proposals reach the funder. The
funder has a **fixed number of grants** and awards them to the highest-scoring
proposals it receives.

Deliberately excluded: reviewer types, panel dynamics, AI. A proposal's chance of
funding is a function of its quality plus noise, and that is the whole of the
evaluation model.

Two things are calibrated against published measurements rather than chosen:

| Parameter | Calibrated against | Value |
|---|---|---|
| evaluation noise | Graves et al. (2011), NHMRC panel-score bootstrap | σ = 0.195 |
| triage accuracy | assumed equal to evaluation accuracy — an internal panel does the same job as a funder panel | σ = 0.195 |

The noise calibration fits **one** parameter to **one** target and reproduces all
three of Graves' published bins:

| | model | Graves |
|---|---|---|
| always funded | 10.9% | 9% |
| sometimes funded | 28.9% | **29%** (fitted) |
| never funded | 60.1% | 61% |

## 2. The central result

Every demand-management mechanism does two things at once, and they pull in
opposite directions:

> **net effect of a cap = triage value − deletion cost**

- **Deletion cost** — what you lose by removing proposals at all. Measured by a
  *placebo arm* that deletes the same number of proposals **at random**. With a
  fixed budget, a smaller pool means the funder digs deeper.
- **Triage value** — what you claw back by deleting *selectively* rather than
  blindly. Measured as the gap between the real mechanism and its volume-matched
  placebo.

**Both are positive and measurable. Triage genuinely works — every mechanism beat
its placebo, in every scenario, at 95%. But it never works well enough.** Even
accurate triage deletes some genuinely excellent proposals (an individual cap of
one forces a researcher with three good ideas to bin two), and under a fixed
budget that loss lands directly on the funded portfolio.

**No mechanism acting on full proposals improved funded-portfolio quality over
doing nothing, in any scenario tested.** Two-stage screening is the exception and
exceeds the uncapped reference in 94% of the robustness cells (§7) — but it does
so by enlarging the pool and screening it twice, not by managing demand, and it
pays for it everywhere else.

## 3. Mechanism comparison

S0, no institutional structure, 20 seeds, 95% CI. `appPrem` is how much better the
*funded researchers* are than the active population; `volAdv` is the funding rate
of high-submission researchers against low-submission ones **at matched quality**,
so above 1 means volume pays off independently of merit.

| mechanism | Qfund | appPrem | volAdv | success | welfare |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| no cap | **1.791±.015** | 1.183±.012 | 1.555±.026 | 7.4% | 0.133 |
| individual K=1 | 1.494±.014 | 1.023±.009 | **1.000±.000** | 26.3% | **0.394** |
| individual K=2 | 1.696±.015 | 1.129±.010 | 1.140±.009 | 14.2% | 0.242 |
| institutional M=30 | 1.760±.017 | **0.480±.010** | 1.604±.017 | 30.0% | 0.221 |
| institutional M=45 | 1.773±.015 | 0.636±.011 | 1.632±.014 | 20.0% | 0.200 |
| cooling off | 1.770±.016 | 1.146±.012 | 1.593±.021 | 10.6% | 0.188 |
| resubmission limit 1 | 1.731±.016 | **1.260±.012** | 1.526±.018 | 10.3% | 0.178 |
| two-stage EOI | 2.274±.019 | 1.100±.015 | 1.590±.019 | 8.7% | 0.152 |

**Welfare here is not capacity-matched** — each mechanism admits a different
number of proposals, so the column rewards whichever cuts volume hardest, and
individual K=1 wins it for that reason alone. Read welfare from the
capacity-matched table in `paper/findings.tex` instead. These figures also
supersede an earlier version of this table: `welfare()` inferred the grant count
as `submitted × payline` when the budget is in fact fixed at 450, which inflated
the uncapped arm ~2× and deflated two-stage ~2×.

### Mechanism by mechanism

**Individual caps** are the only instrument that removes the volume advantage —
exactly 1.000 at K=1, by construction, because everyone gets one shot. K=2
already leaks (1.140). They are otherwise expensive: the largest quality loss of
any non-institutional mechanism, because the cap binds hardest on prolific
researchers who have several good ideas.

**Institutional caps** are the worst instrument on one of the five measures and
the best on one:
- They **destroy applicant-level selectivity** (0.480 against 1.183). They
  concentrate money on fewer people while spreading it across more institutions.
- They are expensive in welfare, because the internal competition discards
  proposals that have already been written. Under capacity matching they hold the
  worst welfare in 59% of parameter settings (§7); two-stage takes it in 68% of
  the rest, when the effort offset ψ is high.
- They are **powerful deconcentrators**. In the heavy-tailed scenario they cut
  institutional HHI from **0.179 to 0.039** — by far the largest effect any
  mechanism has on any metric. This too is a tight-cap property (§7).

**Cooling-off rules** are nearly free. Quality loss is small (−0.017 at a 15%
payline, −0.013 at 5%) and they raise success rates. Implemented with the real
EPSRC trigger: three or more proposals **ranked in the bottom half** — not merely
unfunded — within 24 months, *and* a personal success rate below 25%.

**Resubmission limits** are the **only mechanism that improves applicant-level
selectivity** (1.260 against 1.183), by stopping the same project being ground
through repeatedly. Modelled with near-misses returning preferentially, which is
what makes the limit act on the band where the original decision was least
reliable. This does not appear to be discussed in the literature.

**Two-stage / expression-of-interest** does not manage demand. It subsidises it.
See §5.

## 4. Payline sensitivity

S4 scenario. Triage value is **flat** across paylines; deletion cost **grows** as
the payline loosens.

| payline | mechanism | triage value | deletion cost | net | success |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | cooling off | +0.132 | −0.145 | **−0.013** | 3.4% |
| 5% | institutional M=30 | +0.522 | −0.576 | −0.054 | 10.4% |
| 5% | individual K=1 | +0.278 | −0.505 | −0.227 | 8.6% |
| 25% | cooling off | +0.130 | −0.153 | −0.023 | 18.7% |
| 25% | institutional M=30 | +0.511 | −0.735 | −0.224 | 52.2% |
| 25% | individual K=1 | +0.264 | −0.626 | −0.361 | 45.1% |

**Caps get worse the more money you have**, which is the opposite of the usual
intuition that rationing matters most when things are tight. At very tight
paylines the mild mechanisms are close to free: cooling-off costs essentially
nothing in quality while lifting success from 2.3% to 3.4%.

## 5. Two-stage EOI: the result reversed under scrutiny

Initially the two-stage design looked dominant — same quality as an institutional
cap at a seventh of the wasted effort, because what it discards is the cheap
outline stage.

That was an artefact of holding submission volume fixed. Once entry volume is
allowed to respond to entry cost (an outline costs ~15% of a full proposal):

| volume elasticity | intended proposals | Qfund | volume advantage |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0.00** | 3,799 | **1.741±.032** | 1.70 |
| 0.50 | 10,354 | 2.273±.043 | 1.59 |
| 1.00 | 25,806 | 2.708±.047 | **2.363±.093** |

**At zero elasticity the quality advantage disappears entirely** (1.741 against
no-cap's 1.791). The entire apparent superiority came from drawing more entries,
not from filtering better. At elasticity 1.0 the scheme draws four times the
no-cap volume with a volume advantage of 2.36 — worse than any other mechanism.

A cheap first stage hands the advantage to whoever can enter most often. Barnett
et al. (2015) found that halving the NHMRC application form *increased*
preparation time, which if anything implies a high elasticity.

## 6. Status of the stated hypotheses

| | Hypothesis | Status |
|---|---|---|
| H1 | Institutional caps are lossier than individual caps | **Contradicted at the proposal level** (holds in 97% of settings). Institutional caps give *higher* funded quality: pooling lets them reallocate quota, which individual caps cannot do across a person. **Supported at the applicant level** (96% of settings). The question has no answer without naming the estimand. |
| H2 | Flat institutional caps deconcentrate more than individual caps | **Supported at tight caps** (72% of settings). HHI 0.179 → 0.039 in the heavy-tail scenario. Fails at shallow targets, where individual caps deconcentrate more — a quota that barely binds redistributes nothing. |
| H3 | Individual caps are fairer | **Supported only at tight settings** (69% of settings). volAdv falls to 1.062 at a deep cut, with Qfund collapsing to 1.531, and recovers to 1.457 at loose settings against an uncapped 1.556. The fairness belongs to the setting, not the instrument. |
| H4 | Caps save reviewer cost but waste applicant effort | **Supported.** Institutional quotas hold the worst welfare in 59% of settings, and every instrument beats the uncapped arm, which spends twice the effort for the same 450 grants. Two-stage is the exception: best on welfare at ψ=0, worst as ψ→1. |
| H5 | Individual caps leak via role substitution | **Not yet tested** — circumvention is implemented but not swept. |
| H6 | Cooling-off rules invert their own distributional intent | **Not reproduced.** Roebber & Schultz's case (g) could not be replicated, and our own model does not generate the inversion. Should be dropped unless it appears in the scenario sweep. |

## 7. Robustness scan (exp07, added after the capacity reframe)

424 parameter cells — one-at-a-time plus a 400-point Latin hypercube over
elasticity, ψ, σ_idea, payline, capacity target, triage noise, resubmission
intensity and scenario — re-tuning every instrument in every cell. Full write-up
in `paper/findings.tex` §"Robustness"; raw cells in `code/results/exp07_cells.csv`.

**New result — the feasibility frontier.** Instruments differ in how hard they
can cut at all. Median load floor as a share of uncapped: institutional quotas
0.008, individual caps 0.055, cooling off 0.150, two-stage 0.423, resubmission
limits 0.492. **Only two instruments have a floor they cannot pass**, and for
intrinsic reasons: a resubmission limit can only remove resubmissions (13% reach
at cuts to ≤35%), and a two-stage scheme must screen every outline it attracts
(42% reach; floor 1.53 in the worst case, i.e. it *increases* load). Everything
else reaches any target.

The individual-cap floor was initially reported as 0.28, which was an artefact of
implementing the cap per round. Real schemes are written over several years
("one proposal per investigator every three years"); with a window the floor is
0.055 and reach is 100% at every depth. Fixed 2026-08-04 by adding `window` to
`IndividualCap` plus steady-state initialisation of the rolling window (without
it the whole cohort locks out in unison, which is an initialisation artefact).

**Deep individual caps stop being selection.** At a cut to ≤35%, volAdv falls to
1.062 and Qfund to 1.531 (uncapped 2.031), but the success rate rises to 27%
against 11% at a shallow cut. The cap has taken over the selection and the panel
funds most of what survives — close to an eligibility ballot with a light review.

**Landing ≠ reaching.** Instruments set by small integers land within 8% of target
only 45% (resubmission) to 76% (individual) of the time, against 85–87% for a
quota. Policy granularity is a real constraint.

**The placebo is also a policy.** Random thinning is a partial ballot. It reaches
any target exactly, at precisely the deletion cost and zero triage value.

**Claims that survive (≥92%):** resubmission limits maximise applicant selectivity
(99%); both readings of H1 (97%/96%); two-stage maximises quality and minimises
applicant selectivity (96%/97%); every instrument beats the placebo (92%).

**Claims that break:**
- *institutional quotas worst on welfare* — 59%. Two-stage takes it in 68% of
  failures, driven by ψ: at ψ=0 two-stage has the best welfare of anything
  tested (0.263 median), at ψ→1 the worst (0.119).
- *individual caps minimise volume advantage* — 69%, and only when tight.
- *institutional quotas deconcentrate most* — 72%, failing at shallow targets.

**Elasticity only threatens two-stage.** It moves Qfund by ≤0.12 for every
full-proposal instrument but 0.63 for two-stage, whose load floor rises 0.17 →
0.78 across the range, so at high elasticity two-stage **cannot cut review load
at all**. Since the behavioural case for demand management is an argument that
elasticity is high, two-stage is the instrument least able to do the job on its
proponents' own premises.

**Reliability is a null result.** Round-to-round CV of funded quality is 1.5–2.4%
for everything; too close to rank by. Flat institutional quotas do have the
lowest *between-world* spread (0.057 vs 0.072–0.089).

**What actually moves the answer:** payline (Qfund range 1.084) and within-
researcher idea spread (0.579) dominate; triage noise (0.034), elasticity (0.017)
and ψ (0.000) barely register. The assumption that institutional triage is as
accurate as funder review is therefore not carrying the results.

## 8. What is not yet done

- **Track record / cumulative advantage** — deferred by agreement. Funding does
  not yet raise future funding probability, so the Matthew-effect metrics measure
  static concentration rather than cumulative advantage.
- **Circumvention** (H5) implemented but not swept.
- **Volume elasticity has no empirical anchor.** The two-stage conclusion depends
  on it and it is currently assumed.
- **Effort offset ψ** is set to 0; Barnett (2015) suggests it is close to 1, which
  would make every cap's welfare case worse.
- Replication of Roebber & Schultz is **partial and closed**: case (a) reproduces
  out-of-sample and caught an error in the published paper (the correct-reviewer
  threshold is 105, per their Figure 2, not the 110 implied by their text), but
  the baseline reversal in cases (b)–(g) could not be reproduced from the
  published description.

## 9. What this suggests for the paper

The framing that has emerged is not "which cap is best". It is:

> **Demand management does not improve the science. It redistributes it, and
> every instrument redistributes along a different axis.**

- Individual caps redistribute **from prolific researchers to everyone else**, and
  are the only instrument that stops volume buying funding.
- Institutional caps redistribute **from large institutions to small ones**, at
  the cost of concentrating funding on fewer individuals and the worst welfare of
  any instrument.
- Resubmission limits redistribute **from persistent projects to new ones**, and
  are the only instrument that improves which *people* get funded.
- Two-stage screening redistributes **toward whoever can enter most often**.
- Cooling-off rules redistribute very little, cost very little, and raise success
  rates — which may make them the only instrument that does what it says on the
  tin.

And the practical message: **no cap maximises everything, and the objectives
disagree sharply.** The tightest institutional cap maximises funded-portfolio
quality while having roughly one-eighth the welfare of an individual cap. "What
should the cap be" has no answer until the funder says what it is optimising —
and that, rather than any individual number, is probably the contribution.
